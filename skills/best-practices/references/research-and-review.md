# Reviewing sources and evidence

## Record what affects the decision

For important sources, record the title and link or document location, publisher, relevant date or version, supported claim, and the parts actually examined. Include conditions of use and limitations. Not every source needs extensive notes.

Open the original source when available. Search snippets, summaries, or quotations by others are not enough to establish the full context. If only part of a source is accessible, limit conclusions to that part.

## Check effectiveness claims

Ask:

- What outcome was measured, how was it measured, and what was the comparison?
- Are the starting conditions, scale, time, or resources comparable?
- Are the methods and materials open enough to examine or reproduce?
- Could other factors explain the result?
- Were negative results, failures, or implementation costs also reported?
- Does the result apply to the current task?

Distinguish demonstrations, simulations, personal experience, observational studies, and controlled tests. Each answers different questions. An association between two things does not establish that one causes the other.

## Assess independence and currency

Check who published the source, disclosed commercial relationships, the origin of the data, and whether the claim has evidence that can be examined. Commercial interests do not automatically invalidate evidence. A lack of disclosure does not establish independence either.

Check versions, effective dates, jurisdictions, or technological conditions when relevant. A newer source is not automatically stronger. An older source can remain useful if its conditions still apply.

## Handle differences between sources

Compare the questions each source actually answers. Separate genuine conflicts from differences in definitions, methods, or context. Several publications copying the same source do not provide independent verification.

Explain the choice based on evidence quality and fit with the task. If a conflict remains unresolved, give conditional recommendations or propose a limited trial that can distinguish between the options.

## State confidence

Briefly explain why a recommendation is credible. Do not invent numerical scores without a clear method.

- Strong support means sufficient direct, relevant evidence supports the specific claim.
- Limited support means the evidence is indirect, comes from a single case, or concerns different conditions.
- Unverified means the conclusion is still a hypothesis or an important source has not been examined.

Confidence applies to a specific claim, not to a source's overall reputation. If evidence is lacking, state what is needed to strengthen the decision.
