# Research best practices for the task

Find the practices that best fit the decision at hand. Explain the reasoning, conditions for use, costs or consequences, and how to check results. A source calling something a "best practice" does not prove it is always the best choice.

## 1. Define the decision and context

Use the request, working materials, and existing constraints to identify:

- The task and decision that need research support.
- The desired outcome and measures of success.
- Users, people doing the work, environment, scale, and starting conditions.
- Relevant limits on time, cost, capabilities, tools, versions, or location.
- Risks of failure and requirements that must be met.

Separate requirements, preferences, and assumptions. Ask only about missing information that could materially change the recommendation. Otherwise, state necessary assumptions and proceed.

Do not assume the task concerns content, software, or any particular field. Determine the field from the user's work.

## 2. Define research questions and scope

Turn the goal into questions that can be investigated. Identify available methods, the conditions in which they succeed, known failures, and ways to measure results.

Choose research depth based on the decision's impact, uncertainty, and available time. Light research is enough for small, reversible decisions. High-risk or costly decisions need stronger checks.

Use prior information as a lead. Recheck facts that may have changed. When the user requests research, consult relevant sources. If sources are inaccessible, explain the limits and do not present recalled information as new research.

## 3. Find sources suited to the field

Choose where and how to search based on the question. Do not rely on one site or source type for every task.

- Use standards, regulations, official documentation, or original sources to establish requirements and stated capabilities.
- Use research, tests that can be examined, and independent evaluations to assess effectiveness.
- Use case studies and practitioner accounts to understand implementation, obstacles, and real-world conditions.
- Use forums, reviews, and popular examples as leads for problems or approaches that need verification.

Judge a source by its relevance and the evidence it provides for a claim. Official documentation can establish support for a feature, but may not establish that a product is more effective than alternatives.

Look for evidence against the initial approach. Do not collect only sources that repeat the same advice. Trace claims to their original sources. Several articles citing one study still rely on one body of evidence.

Explore other fields when the mechanism or problem is similar. Explain why a practice may transfer and which differences in context limit its use.

Read the [evidence review guide](references/research-and-review.md) when comparing sources, checking effectiveness claims, or dealing with conflicting findings.

## 4. Assess evidence and fit

For each practice that could affect the decision, check:

- The problem it addresses and the evidence that it helps.
- The conditions of the study or implementation, including comparisons and outcome measures.
- Its fit with the task's scale, resources, environment, and constraints.
- Prerequisites, implementation costs, consequences, and conditions in which it should not be used.
- How current the source is, commercial interests, and anything that remains unverified.

Distinguish mandatory requirements, evidence-based recommendations, practitioner conventions, preferences, and hypotheses that need testing. Do not present common habits as obligations.

Popularity, reputation, awards, or user numbers do not automatically establish effectiveness. Separate observed outcomes, explanations of their causes, and what remains unknown. Do not invent quotes, numbers, experiences, or test results.

When sources conflict, examine differences in goals, methods, versions, populations, and conditions of use. Explain any unresolved conflict. Do not choose based on article counts alone.

## 5. Make usable recommendations

Choose practices that best support the goal. Each important recommendation should explain the action, reasoning and sources, conditions for use, and how to check the result. Include prerequisites or consequences when they affect the decision.

Prioritize by impact, strength of evidence, and implementation effort. Offer alternatives when the choice depends on different conditions. If the evidence is insufficient to choose, propose a limited trial with clear measures of success.

Match the output format to the need. A short recommendation may need only a few bullets. A complex comparison may benefit from a table. Do not impose a long report or a fixed source count.

Follow the user's or organization's language and style guidance when available. Preserve the author's voice and details needed to understand or use the result.

### When reviewing existing work

Start with a brief assessment. Order findings by impact, locate each problem, and suggest a concrete correction with its supporting reasoning. Distinguish problems from matters of taste. State what was not checked. Do not change the work if the user requested only a review.

When delivering a finished artifact, keep unsolicited alternatives and process notes outside it. This does not restrict concrete corrections or alternatives needed in a requested review.

## 6. Stop when the evidence is sufficient for the decision

Research is sufficient when the important questions have answers, the main recommendations have relevant support, conflicts that could change the decision have been addressed, and gaps in the evidence are explicit. No source count guarantees sufficiency.

Stop adding references when they no longer affect the decision. If time or access prevents an important check, report provisional findings and the evidence still needed. Do not present a recommendation as certain while major uncertainty remains.

## 7. Check the result and limits of application

Before saving or delivering the result:

- Confirm that the recommendations address the user's task and are feasible within its constraints.
- Link important claims to supporting sources. Note versions or dates when they affect applicability.
- Distinguish evidence read, direct observations, inferences, and results actually tested.
- State the scope of the checks and limitations that affect the decision.
- When the result promotes an owned product, sponsor, or affiliate offer, disclose the relationship clearly.
- When reusing third-party images, music, excerpts, or other works, check permission or licensing and include attribution where required. If rights cannot be established, flag the gap and use an authorized alternative.
- For reader-facing materials, check relevant accessibility needs, such as captions, alternative text, contrast, and text size.
- Review the artifact as someone who has not read the conversation. Remove unnecessary chat history, internal instructions, names, and temporary examples.

If implementation is also requested, apply the selected practices within the authorized scope. Verify outcomes against the measures of success, rather than treating completed steps as proof of success. If the task was research only, explain how to validate the recommendations without claiming to have tested them.

This skill grants no additional permission to change systems, send messages, publish, buy, or take external actions. Follow the applicable task boundaries and permissions.
